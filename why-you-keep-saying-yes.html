Why You Keep Saying Yes When You Mean No

It's not kindness. It's something older than that.

← Back to Blog
By Jess Hollamby August 2026 7 min read

You said yes to something this week that you didn't want to do.

You knew while you were saying it. You felt the no sitting right there, clear and certain. And then you heard yourself say yes anyway - and spent the rest of the day quietly resenting it, or quietly resenting yourself for agreeing, or both.

This isn't about being a pushover. It's not about being weak. And it is absolutely not a character flaw. It's a pattern - and it usually started somewhere much earlier than you think.

It was never really about being nice

People-pleasing gets dressed up as kindness. And sometimes it is kind - but when it's a pattern you can't seem to stop, when you say yes before you've even checked in with yourself, when the thought of someone being disappointed by you produces genuine dread - that's not kindness. That's something else.

What it usually is: a very old survival strategy.

At some point - often in childhood, sometimes in a relationship, sometimes just through years of learning what was safe - you figured out that making others comfortable made you more secure. That keeping the peace felt better than the cost of disrupting it. That being easy and agreeable and endlessly available kept things calm.

Your nervous system learned that lesson and kept applying it. Even when you're an adult. Even when the original threat is long gone. Even when the people around you now would probably be completely fine with you saying no.

Signs the pattern is running

  • You say yes and feel the resentment almost immediately
  • You apologise for things that aren't your fault
  • You minimise your own needs to avoid being "difficult"
  • You feel responsible for other people's emotional states
  • Saying no - even to small things - feels like a much bigger deal than it should
  • You wait to see what others want before expressing what you want
  • You've been told you're "so easy-going" but inside you're managing a constant calculation
  • You're exhausted in a way that doesn't match what you've actually done

That last one. The exhaustion that doesn't add up. That's the tell.

Managing other people's comfort takes energy. Running a constant background check on whether everyone is okay, whether you're taking up too much space, whether you've said something wrong - that is work. Invisible work. And it compounds.

What's actually underneath it

Under most people-pleasing patterns, you'll find some version of the same thing: a fear that your needs are too much, that expressing them will cost you something, that the real you - the one with wants and limits and opinions - won't be acceptable.

This is especially common in empaths. When you've spent years absorbing everyone else's emotional state, you get very good at reading what people need. The logical extension of that, when it goes unchecked, is managing those needs before they're even expressed. You start to disappear into everyone else's experience.

And then one day you realise you don't know what you actually want. You've been so busy tracking everyone else that you've lost the signal of your own.

The part nobody talks about

Changing this pattern is not about suddenly becoming someone who doesn't care what others think. It's slower and quieter than that.

It starts with learning to feel the no before it becomes a yes. Noticing the moment your body contracts slightly, or the warmth drains out of you, or the yes comes out flat. That's the signal. You don't have to act on it immediately - but noticing it is the beginning of something.

The goal isn't to become someone who says no to everything. The goal is to start making choices that are actually yours - ones you can stand behind, that come from a genuine place, that leave you feeling solid rather than hollowed out.

That's different from what most people are running.

Where the work usually starts

The pattern lives in the nervous system, not just the mind. You can understand it intellectually and still feel the same pull toward yes every single time. That's not a failure - it's just how deeply wired this stuff gets.

It doesn't disappear overnight. But it does change. And the first step is usually just - finally seeing it clearly.
